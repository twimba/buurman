Landlord-tenant law in the United States is mainly the law of the state, and in many places also of the county or city, in which the dwelling is located; federal law adds disclosure and anti-discrimination rules. This agreement is a general form. Where a statute or ordinance that cannot be waived by agreement conflicts with this agreement, the statute or ordinance prevails, and the parties must check the law that is in force when they sign and while the tenancy lasts.

This agreement sets out selected rules of the state (or of the District of Columbia) in which the dwelling is located; it does not set out county or city ordinances, which may give the tenant further protection.

The rules of the state in which the dwelling is located are not set out in this agreement. The parties must check them, in particular the limits on security deposits and late fees, the notice periods for rent increases and for ending the tenancy, any just-cause or rent-stabilization rules and the disclosures that state law requires.

The tenancy starts on start date.

The tenancy is for a fixed term ending on end date. If the tenant remains in possession after the end of the fixed term with the landlord’s consent and no new agreement is made, the tenancy continues from month to month on the terms of this agreement, unless the law of the state provides otherwise.

The tenancy is periodic (month-to-month) and continues from period to period until it is ended in accordance with this agreement and the law of the state.

In several states the landlord may not end the tenancy, or refuse to renew it, without a cause that the law recognizes, even at the end of a fixed term (see article ).

In Washington, the landlord may not charge more than a 5% difference in rent for this dwelling depending on whether the agreement is month-to-month or for a specified period, and may not make the terms of payment or other material conditions more burdensome for 1 type of agreement than for the other (RCW 59.18.700, subsection (4)).

Changes to the rent are governed by article .

During a fixed term the rent may be changed only by written agreement of the parties. During a periodic (month-to-month) tenancy the landlord may change the rent by written notice served in the manner and with the notice period that the law of the state requires, and only within any limit that state or local law sets.

Where Civil Code § 1947.12 applies (it does not apply, among others, to housing issued a certificate of occupancy within the previous 15 years, to certain separately alienable dwellings whose owner is not a real estate investment trust, a corporation or a limited liability company with a corporate member and who has given the tenant the statutory exemption notice, and to the other housing listed in subdivision (d) of that section), the owner may not, over any 12-month period, increase the gross rental rate by more than 5% plus the percentage change in the cost of living, or 10%, whichever is lower, of the lowest gross rental rate charged for the dwelling at any time during the 12 months before the effective date of the increase, and not in more than 2 increments over that period. That section is repealed on 1 January 2030. A local rent ordinance may set a lower limit.

For a periodic tenancy, notice of an increase of 10% or less (counting all increases in the 12 months before its effective date) must be delivered at least 30 days before the increase takes effect, and notice of a greater increase at least 90 days before, except that 30 days apply where the increase is caused by a change in the tenant’s income or family composition determined by a recertification required by statute or regulation (Civil Code § 827, subdivision (b)); service by mail extends these periods as the Code of Civil Procedure provides.

In Oregon, during a tenancy other than week-to-week, the landlord may not increase the rent during the first year of the tenancy, may not increase it more than once in any 12-month period, must give the tenant written notice at least 90 days before the effective date stating the amount of the increase, the new rent and the effective date, and may not increase it by more than the maximum percentage that the Oregon Department of Administrative Services publishes each year as the lesser of 10% or 7% plus the consumer price index change set out in ORS 90.324 (ORS 90.323, subsection (2)). The percentage limit does not apply where the first certificate of occupancy was issued less than 15 years before the date of the notice, or to certain regulated affordable housing under the conditions of ORS 90.323, subsection (5).

In Washington, unless an exemption of RCW 59.18.710 applies (for example a dwelling whose first certificate of occupancy was issued 12 or fewer years before the date of the notice), the landlord may not increase the rent during the first 12 months of the tenancy, nor during any 12-month period by more than 7% plus the consumer price index, or 10%, whichever is less (RCW 59.18.700, subsection (1)). The landlord must give each affected tenant at least 90 days’ prior written notice of any increase, in the form prescribed by RCW 59.18.720, and an increase may not take effect before the end of the term of the rental agreement (RCW 59.18.140, subsection (3)); for a subsidized tenancy whose rent is based on the income of the tenant or other circumstances specific to the subsidized household, the notice period is 30 days. These rules expire on 1 July 2040.

In New York, if the landlord intends to renew the tenancy with a rent increase of 5% or more, the landlord must give written notice at least 30 days before, where the tenant has occupied the dwelling for less than 1 year and the lease term is less than 1 year; at least 60 days before, where the occupancy or the lease term is at least 1 year but less than 2 years; and at least 90 days before, where the occupancy or the lease term is at least 2 years (Real Property Law § 226-c). Without timely notice the tenancy continues under the existing terms until the notice period has run.

Where the dwelling is covered by the Good Cause Eviction Law (Real Property Law Article 6-A, which applies in New York City and in localities that adopt it, with the exemptions of that Article, for example for small landlords), a rent increase in a calendar year greater than the local rent standard (5% plus the annual change in the consumer price index, or 10%, whichever is lower) is presumed to be unreasonable, a presumption the landlord may rebut on the grounds listed in Real Property Law § 216. Rent-stabilized and rent-controlled apartments are governed by their own rules and orders.

In the District of Columbia, for a rental unit subject to the rent stabilization program of the Rental Housing Act of 1985, an increase in the rent charged for an occupied unit may not exceed the adjustment of general applicability plus 2%, and never more than 10%; for a unit occupied by an elderly tenant or a tenant with a disability, D.C. Code § 42-3502.24 applies instead (D.C. Code § 42-3502.08, subsection (h)). A rent increase does not take effect until the first day on which rent is normally paid that occurs more than 60 days after the notice of the increase is given to the tenant (D.C. Code § 42-3509.04, subsection (b)).

New Jersey has no statewide limit on rent increases, but many municipalities have rent control ordinances. A tenant covered by the Anti-Eviction Act may be evicted for failing to pay a rent increase only if the increase is not unconscionable, complies with any applicable rent control ordinance and was preceded by the required notice (N.J.S.A. 2A:18-61.1).

Maryland has no statewide limit on rent increases; county or city rent stabilization laws may apply, for example in Montgomery County, Prince George’s County and Takoma Park.

Maine has no statewide limit on rent increases; a city ordinance may apply, for example the rent control ordinance of the City of Portland.

Minnesota has no statewide limit on rent increases; a city ordinance may apply, for example the rent stabilization ordinance of the City of Saint Paul.

Some states and cities limit the amount or frequency of rent increases (for example California, Oregon and Washington statewide, and rent-stabilized housing in New York and the District of Columbia); where such a limit applies to the dwelling, it prevails over this agreement.

California law sets no fixed maximum for residential late fees; California courts have treated them as liquidated damages, so a late fee should be a reasonable estimate of the landlord’s loss from late payment (an interpretation of case law, not a statutory figure).

In New York no late fee may be charged unless the rent has not been paid within 5 days of the date it was due, and the fee may not exceed USD 50 or 5% of the monthly rent, whichever is less (Real Property Law § 238-a, subdivision 2).

If the landlord has not received the rent within 5 days of the due date, the landlord sends the tenant, by certified mail, a written notice of the failure to receive it (Real Property Law § 235-e, subdivision (d)). For rent paid in cash or by any instrument other than the tenant’s personal check, the landlord gives the tenant a written receipt (subdivision (a)).

In the District of Columbia a late fee may not exceed 5% of the full amount of rent due and may be charged only if this agreement states its maximum amount and the tenant has not paid the full rent within 5 days, or any longer grace period stated here, after the due date; interest may not be charged on a late fee, a late fee may be imposed only once on each late payment, and nonpayment of a late fee is not a ground for eviction (D.C. Code § 42-3505.31).

In Massachusetts no interest or penalty for failure to pay rent may be imposed until 30 days after the rent was due (G.L. c. 186, § 15B, subsection (1)(c)).

In Maryland a penalty for late payment of rent may not exceed 5% of the amount of unpaid rent due for the rental period for which the payment was delinquent; where rent is paid in weekly instalments the penalty is limited as Real Property § 8-208, subsection (d)(3)(ii), provides.

In Maine a payment of rent is late only if it is not made within 15 days from the time it is due, and the penalty may not exceed 4% of the amount due for 1 month; it may be charged only if the landlord gave the tenant written notice, when this agreement was entered into, that a penalty of up to 4% of 1 month’s rent may be charged for late payment, and this clause is that notice (14 M.R.S. § 6028).

In Minnesota a late fee may be charged only if the parties have agreed in writing that a late fee may be imposed and when it will be imposed, and it may not exceed 8% of the overdue rent payment (Minn. Stat. § 504B.177).

In New Jersey a tenant who is a senior citizen receiving a Social Security old age pension, a railroad retirement pension or another governmental pension in lieu of it, or who receives Social Security disability benefits, Supplemental Security Income or benefits under Work First New Jersey, has a grace period of 5 business days when the rent is due on the first of the month, and no delinquency or late charge may be assessed during it (N.J.S.A. 2A:42-6.1).

In Oregon a late charge may be imposed only if the rent is not received by the 4th day of the rental period and this agreement specifies the obligation, the type and amount of the charge and the dates on which rent and the charge are due; the charge may not exceed a reasonable flat amount charged once per rental period, a reasonable daily amount from the 5th day of no more than 6% of that flat amount, or 5% of the periodic rent for each 5-day period of delinquency from the 5th day (ORS 90.260).

In Washington no late fee may be charged for rent paid within 5 days following its due date; if rent is more than 5 days past due, late fees may be charged from the first day after the due date until paid (RCW 59.18.170, subsection (2)).
