Residential tenancies in Canada are governed by the law of the province or territory in which the dwelling is located, not by federal law. The rules on prescribed forms, deposits, rent increases, notices and termination differ materially between provinces and territories, and some of them prescribe a mandatory lease form or standard terms that apply despite this agreement: in Ontario the Standard Form of Lease, in Quebec the lease form of the Tribunal administratif du logement, in British Columbia the standard terms of the Residential Tenancy Regulation. This document is not such a form. The parties must check the law of the province or territory concerned before signing.

Where a provision of the applicable provincial or territorial law that cannot be excluded or varied by agreement conflicts with this agreement, that provision prevails.

A lessee entitled to maintain occupancy is entitled, as of right, to the renewal of a fixed-term lease when it ends; the lease is renewed on the same conditions and for the same term or, where the initial term exceeds 12 months, for 12 months, unless the parties agree on a different renewal term (article 1941).

In British Columbia, if on the date specified as the end of a fixed-term tenancy the landlord and the tenant have not entered into a new tenancy agreement, they are deemed to have renewed the tenancy agreement on the same terms as a periodic tenancy from month to month, unless the agreement validly requires the tenant to vacate at the end of the term (section 44, subsection 3). Such a requirement is permitted only where the landlord is an individual who, or whose close family member, will occupy the rental unit at the end of the term (section 13, subsection 2, of the Residential Tenancy Act and section 13.1 of the Residential Tenancy Regulation).

The landlord may increase the rent only by giving the tenant at least 90 days’ written notice in the form approved by the Landlord and Tenant Board, stating the amount of the new rent (section 116, subsections 1 and 3, of the Residential Tenancies Act, 2006); an increase without that notice is void (section 116, subsection 4). During the tenancy the increase may not exceed the guideline that the Minister determines for each calendar year, which is not more than 2.5 per cent, except as permitted by sections 121, 123, 126 or 127 of that Act (section 120, subsections 1 and 2). The guideline does not apply where no part of the building in which the unit is located was occupied for residential purposes on or before November 15, 2018 (section 6.1, subsection 2, of that Act, which also covers additions and, under subsection 3, certain units created in houses).

A clause providing for the readjustment of the rent during the lease is without effect in a fixed-term lease of 12 months or less; in a lease of more than 12 months it is without effect during the first 12 months or if it provides for more than 1 readjustment in each period of 12 months (article 1906 of the Civil Code of Quebec).

On the renewal of the lease the lessor may modify its conditions, in particular the term or the rent, only by giving the lessee a notice of modification at least 3 months but not more than 6 months before the end of the term; for a lease of less than 12 months, at least 1 month but not more than 2 months before the end of the term; for a lease of indeterminate term, at least 1 month but not more than 2 months before the modification; for the lease of a room, at least 10 days but not more than 20 days (article 1942). A lessee who refuses the modification must notify the lessor within 1 month after receiving the notice, failing which the lessee is deemed to have accepted the renewal on the proposed conditions (article 1945); the lessor may then apply to the Tribunal administratif du logement within 1 month after receiving the refusal to have the rent fixed, failing which the lease is renewed on the previous conditions (article 1947).

Neither party may apply to the tribunal to fix the rent or modify another condition of the lease of a dwelling leased by a housing cooperative, or of a dwelling in a newly erected building or a building recently converted to rental use, during the 5 years following the date on which the building is ready for its intended use (article 1955). The lessor may invoke these restrictions against the lessee only if they are stated in the lease and, for a new or converted building, only if the lease states the maximum rent that the lessor may charge during those 5 years; for such a dwelling, a lessee who refuses the proposed modification must leave the dwelling at the end of the lease (article 1945).

The landlord may not impose a rent increase for at least 12 months after the date on which the rent was first payable or, if the rent has been increased before, after the effective date of the last increase (section 42, subsection 1, of the Residential Tenancy Act). The landlord must give the tenant notice of the increase in the approved form at least 3 months before its effective date (section 42, subsections 2 and 3).

The increase may not exceed the amount calculated under the Residential Tenancy Regulation (the inflation rate, being the 12-month average percent change in the all-items Consumer Price Index for British Columbia), an amount ordered by the director or an amount agreed to by the tenant in writing (section 43, subsection 1); the tenant may deduct an increase collected in breach of these rules from the rent (section 43, subsection 5).

The rent may be increased only in the manner, with the written notice, at the intervals and within the limits that the law of the province or territory in which the dwelling is located permits. Most provinces and territories require written notice of several months and allow no more than 1 increase in 12 months; some limit the amount of the increase.
